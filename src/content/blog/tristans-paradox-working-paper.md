There is a curious little truth tucked into the folds of human experience: in
our youth, we seem to understand the opposite sex better than we understand
ourselves. While still fumbling through our own development, we somehow hold
doctorates in romantic reconnaissance — boyish experts on girlhood, girlish
professors on manhood — all before we've worked out what we're doing here.

I've given that regularity a name — **Tristan's Paradox** — and written it up as
a short working paper, now archived with a permanent, citable DOI. This post is
the plain-English doorway; the paper is the deeper dive.

## The paradox, in one line

Let *O(t)* be your competence at reading the opposite sex and *S(t)* your
self-knowledge, both changing with age. The paradox is simply that the two
curves **cross**:

> In youth, *O(t) > S(t)*. In maturity, *S(t) > O(t)*.

The claim isn't that our grasp of the other sex collapses — it's that the
*ordering* flips. The attention and instinct that once poured outward toward the
other gradually turn inward, toward the self and its purpose. Competence arrives
in each domain precisely as its usefulness for courtship recedes. Nature's
grand joke, really.

## What's in the paper

The working paper sketches three reasons youth reads the other so fluently
(instinct over strategy, authenticity as signal, undivided outward attention),
the "watcher-watched" game of attention in a crowded room, the tension between
the modern "fix yourself first" mantra and how people actually choose partners,
and a closing reframe of maturity as *anti-fragility* — the spirit of sparring
gently rather than going to war. Passion may win the round, but only purpose
wins the game.
